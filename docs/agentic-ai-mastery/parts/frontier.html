Orientation

Every chapter ended with a "what is missing" section. This chapter gathers those gaps into a coherent picture of the frontier — not a list of bugs, but the honest boundary of what this architecture, and agentic AI generally, cannot yet do well. A field manual that only described what works would be a sales brochure; the gaps are where the interesting work is.

The gaps cluster into five themes. Each is a place where the current answer is "hand-tuned rules" or "not yet built", and where the next generation of agent platforms will differentiate.

The frontier

Learned decisions replacing hand-tuned rules

The platform is full of hand-written rule tables — pattern assembly, RAG strategy selection, model routing, retention. The frontier is replacing them with decisions learned from measured outcomes.

Mechanism

Today's decision points use ordered rule tables that a human wrote and tunes: the pattern assembler (chapter 1), adaptive RAG selection (chapter 2), per-role model routing (chapter 16). These are auditable and predictable, which is why they exist — but they are also static and hand-tuned. The frontier is a bandit or learned policy over each, optimising measured success-per-dollar per goal class, with the eval and experiment machinery (chapters 9–10) providing the training signal. The tension is auditability: a learned policy is harder to explain to a regulator than a rule table.

Where it would attach

  • app/agent/pattern_assembler.pylearned pattern selection
  • app/rag/agentic/retrieval_policy.pylearned RAG strategy selection
  • app/ai_router/role_policy.pylearned model routing
  • app/intelligence/experiment_registry.pythe training signal (chapter 9)

The gap

today   the assembler applies a hand-written rule table (chapter 1)
frontier a bandit over pattern sets, learning which combination gives the
         best success-per-dollar for each goal class, from measured outcomes
blocker  the eval/experiment machinery exists but is not wired to decisions;
         and learned policies are harder to audit than rule tables

Why it matters

Rated 4/8 — barely implemented, highly valuable. Hand-tuned rules are where a lot of the platform's remaining cost and quality sits: the assembler over-provisions patterns "to be safe", routing is conservative, retrieval selection is coarse. Learned decisions would close that gap, and the infrastructure to train them already exists. The open question is governance — how to keep a learned policy explainable enough for a regulated deployment.

The risk

  • Unexplainable decisions. A learned policy a regulator cannot audit.
  • Reward hacking. Optimising a proxy (cost) against quality.

The path

  • Wire evals to decisions, start with the lowest-risk (model routing), keep a rule-table fallback.

Privacy-preserving cross-tenant learning

Every tenant learns alone. The frontier is aggregating lessons, failure modes, and attack patterns across tenants without leaking any tenant's data — the largest unrealised value in the platform.

Mechanism

Memory, reflexion lessons, red-team findings, and retrieval quality all improve per tenant in isolation (chapters 3, 9, 12) — which means common failure modes are rediscovered independently by every tenant. The frontier is privacy-preserving aggregation: federated learning, differential privacy, or careful anonymisation that lets the platform learn "this class of goal often fails this way" or "this injection pattern is circulating" across the fleet without any tenant's data reaching another. The hard part is proving the privacy guarantee.

Where it would attach

  • app/memory/*cross-tenant lesson aggregation (chapter 3)
  • app/enterprise/red_team_corpus.pyshared attack intelligence (chapter 12)
  • app/evals/dataset_builder.pyshared failure patterns (chapter 10)

The gap

today   tenant A discovers an injection pattern; tenants B-Z stay vulnerable
        until each is attacked independently
frontier the pattern hardens the detectors for everyone, without A's data
         leaving A
blocker  the privacy guarantee must be provable, not hoped

Why it matters

Rated 3/7 — essentially unbuilt, genuinely valuable. Security is the clearest case: an attack seen once should protect everyone, immediately. But the same applies to failure modes and retrieval quality. The reason it is unbuilt is that the privacy bar is high and the failure is catastrophic — a cross-tenant leak dressed as learning ends contracts. It is a frontier precisely because the value is large and the safety requirement is absolute.

The risk

  • Cross-tenant leakage. The catastrophic failure this must never cause.
  • Weak anonymisation. Re-identification from aggregated lessons.

The path

  • Start with the safest signal (attack patterns, which are not tenant data), prove the guarantee, then expand.

Native multimodal and richer perception

Multimodal is extract-to-text today. The frontier is native reasoning over pixels, audio, and video — and the joint embeddings, cross-modal grounding, and speech-to-speech that come with it.

Mechanism

The current path captions images, transcribes audio, and reduces everything to text (chapter 15) — pragmatic, citable, governable, and lossy. The frontier is native multimodal: joint image/text embeddings for visual search (chapter 7), cross-modal grounding (verifying a claim against a chart, chapter 18), speech-to-speech voice without the STT/TTS round-trip (chapter 26), and true document understanding where layout is meaning. Each is more capable and harder to cite and govern, which is why the production path stays extract-to-text.

Where it would attach

  • app/embedding/router.pynative joint embeddings (chapter 7)
  • app/perception/multimodal.pynative perception (chapter 15)
  • app/voice/*speech-to-speech (chapter 26)

The gap

today   a diagram → caption + OCR → text → retrieve/reason (lossy)
frontier reason over the diagram's pixels directly, cite the region, ground
         a claim against a chart
blocker  citation and governance are harder for native modalities than for
         extracted text

Why it matters

Rated 4/5 — the production path (extraction) is sound and the frontier is genuinely better only for specific uses (visual search, document understanding, low-latency voice). It is a real frontier but a narrower one than the others — for most workloads, extract-to-text will remain the right, governable choice. The interesting cases are where layout or visual detail is the information, and extraction demonstrably loses it.

The risk

  • Uncitable claims. Native reasoning without a verifiable source.
  • Ungovernable modalities. Audio/video harder to scan and redact.

The path

  • Adopt where extraction demonstrably loses information; keep extraction as the governable default.

Formal guarantees and verifiable safety

Safety today is enforced by controls and measured by evals. The frontier is provable guarantees — checkable arguments that a loop terminates, that a scope cannot leak, that a control cannot be bypassed.

Mechanism

The platform enforces safety through deterministic controls (chapter 12), scope composition (chapter 14), and append-only invariants (chapter 1), and measures it through evals and red teaming (chapters 10, 31). These are strong but empirical — they show safety holds on the cases tested. The frontier is formal: a checkable proof that the agent loop must terminate, that the scope intersection cannot leak, that a fail-closed control cannot degrade to fail-open under any path. Formal methods for AI systems are early, but the highest-risk autonomous flows are where they would matter most.

Where it would attach

  • app/agent/patterns/loop_engineering.pytermination proofs (chapter 1)
  • app/orchestration/runtime_profile_builder.pyscope-composition proofs (chapter 14)
  • app/policy_runtime/compiler.pyprovable policy invariants (chapter 13)

The gap

today   iteration caps enforce termination numerically; tests show scopes
        don't leak on the cases tried
frontier a checkable argument that the loop MUST terminate, that a tenant-A
         request CANNOT return a tenant-B row under any path
blocker  formal methods for non-deterministic AI systems are immature

Why it matters

Rated 3/6 — unbuilt, and valuable specifically for the highest-risk autonomous flows. For most goals, empirical safety (controls plus evals) is proportionate. But for an agent that can move money or delete data autonomously, a proof that a control cannot be bypassed is worth more than a thousand passing tests. This is the longest-horizon frontier, gated on the maturity of formal methods for AI, but it is where the ceiling of trustworthy autonomy ultimately sits.

The risk

  • False confidence. A proof of the wrong property.
  • Proving the model, not the system. Guarantees that do not cover the LLM's behaviour.

The path

  • Start with the deterministic parts (loop termination, scope composition) where proofs are tractable.

The state of the art, honestly

This platform is a genuinely complete production agent system — and it is complete in the way a first-generation system is: the capabilities exist, the hard problems have real answers, and the next generation of work is optimisation, learning, and formal rigour on top.

The assessment

Across 33 chapters and 300+ concepts, the pattern is consistent: the capabilities are shipped and governed (agent loop, retrieval, memory, guardrails, governance, observability all rate high), while the intelligence that would make those capabilities self-improving is where the gaps cluster (learned decisions, cross-tenant learning, formal guarantees all rate low). That is the honest shape of the current state of the art in production agentic AI: we know how to build agents that plan, retrieve, act, verify, and stay governed and safe; we are still learning how to make them get better on their own without giving up the auditability that makes them deployable.

What to take from this

If you are building an agent platform, this book is a map of the problems you will face and real answers to most of them — the capability layer is well-understood and this system implements it thoroughly. If you are researching, the low-rated concepts are the open problems worth your time. And if you are an architect deciding whether to build or buy: the hard, unglamorous 80% (isolation, idempotency, rollback, audit, cost control, degraded mode) is exactly what separates a demo from a platform, and it is exactly what a first survey underestimates. That 80% is most of this book.

The honest caveat

  • Ratings are one engineer's reading of the code as it stands, not a benchmark.
  • The frontier moves. Today's low-rated concept is next year's table stakes.
