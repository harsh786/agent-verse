Orientation

The patterns in chapter 1 coordinate agents for the duration of one goal. This chapter is about something more persistent: standing teams of agents organised like a company — departments, roles, reporting lines, autonomy levels, and shared organisational memory — that exist across goals and learn as an organisation, not just as individuals.

The distinction matters because a team is a governance object, not just an execution pattern. A department has a budget, a memory scope, an autonomy tier, and an accountability structure; forming one is closer to hiring than to spawning. AgentVerse implements this in two related layers: the org layer (departments, roles, team formation) and the more experimental civilization layer (long-lived agent societies with reputation and a constitution).

The org layer

Departments and roles

Agents are organised into departments with roles, mirroring a company — so a goal routes to the department that owns its domain, and access follows the org structure.

Mechanism

A department groups agents by business function (finance, engineering, support) with a role taxonomy (analyst, approver, lead) that maps to permissions and to what each agent may do. Department scope bounds memory (chapter 3) and knowledge access, so finance agents share finance context and do not see engineering's. The structure is data, so an organisation can be reshaped without code.

Finance department
 roles     analyst (submit/read), approver (act on approvals), lead (admin)
 agents    invoice-agent, reconcile-agent, forecast-agent
 memory    acme/finance/* — shared across the three, invisible to support
 access    the tax-policy collection, the ERP connector
a "reconcile payouts" goal routes here; a "triage this bug" goal routes to Eng

At production scale

Department structure is what most enterprises actually want and what most agent frameworks lack — a middle scope between "one global agent" and "isolated per-agent". It makes a new agent useful immediately (it inherits the department's memory and access) and it makes access reviewable along familiar org lines.

Failure modes

  • Scope creep. Department memory promoted to global "because it's useful".
  • Orphaned departments. A reorg leaving memory with no owner.

Choose it when

  • Multiple agents serve distinct business functions.

Team formation

For a goal too big for one agent or one department, a formation engine assembles a team from the capability registry — picking agents whose skills cover the goal and defining how they coordinate.

Mechanism

The formation engine reads the goal's requirements, queries the capability registry for agents that can cover them, and produces a team manifest: which agents, what each is responsible for, and the coordination pattern (supervisor, debate, group chat). It is the meta-agent idea (chapter 9) applied to teams — natural language in, a governed team configuration out, reviewed before it runs.

goal "investigate and remediate the checkout latency regression"

At production scale

Team formation is powerful and needs the same guardrails as spawning: a cap on team size, a budget split across members, and review before a formed team acts on real systems. Its value is matching capability to need automatically; its risk is a formed team that is larger and more expensive than the goal warranted.

Failure modes

  • Oversized teams. More agents than the goal needs.
  • Capability mismatch. An agent on the team lacking the tool the manifest assumed.
  • Unreviewed formation. A team acting without approval.

Choose it when

  • Goals span capabilities no single agent has.

Meta-orchestration

The layer above departments: routes goals to the right department or team, coordinates cross-department work, and manages the org-scale equivalent of the agent loop.

Mechanism

The meta-orchestrator handles goals that need more than one department — "prepare the board pack" touches finance, product, and risk. It decomposes across departments, routes each part to the owning team, monitors progress, and synthesises. It is the supervisor pattern (chapter 1) lifted to the organisational level, with departments as the units instead of individual agents.

At production scale

Meta-orchestration is where the org model earns its complexity: cross-functional work that no single department owns. It also inherits every risk of multi-agent coordination amplified — loop detection and failure management at the org level exist because an org of agents can deadlock or spiral just as a team can. Keep the same caps and budgets that bound spawning (chapter 1).

Failure modes

  • Cross-department deadlock. Two departments waiting on each other.
  • Synthesis loss. Rich department output flattened.
  • Unbounded org-level fan-out. Cost blowout across departments.

Choose it when

  • Goals genuinely span multiple business functions.

Autonomy tiers

How much an agent or department may do without a human — from supervised (every action gated) to bounded-autonomous (act within limits) to autonomous (act freely within scope) — set per department and enforced by the runtime.

Mechanism

An autonomy tier determines the default gating: supervised agents route every consequential action to a human; bounded-autonomous agents act within budget and risk limits, escalating only above them; autonomous agents act freely within their scope. The tier is a governance setting, not an agent choice, and it interacts with tool risk — even an autonomous agent's destructive actions are gated (chapter 13).

Worked example

tier             behaviour
supervised       every write gated → finance dept during quarter close
bounded          act ≤ $50 and ≤ risk medium, escalate above → support triage
autonomous       act freely within scope, destructive still gated → internal

a new department starts supervised and earns autonomy as it proves reliable

At production scale

Autonomy tiers are how you deploy agents at all: start supervised, measure reliability, and raise the tier as trust is earned — the same way you would with a new employee. The invariant that survives every tier is that irreversible and destructive actions are gated regardless; autonomy governs the reversible middle, not the dangerous extremes.

Failure modes

  • Autonomy without earned trust. A new agent acting freely before it is proven.
  • Tier overriding tool risk. An autonomous agent's destructive action ungated.

Choose it when

  • Deploying agents that take real actions — set the tier deliberately.

Organisational learning

A team learns as an organisation: lessons, preferences, and procedures accumulate at the department and org level, so the whole team benefits from any member's experience.

Mechanism

Org learning aggregates individual agents' lessons (chapter 3) into department- and org-scoped memory, with the same corroboration and decay discipline. A procedure discovered by one finance agent becomes available to all finance agents; a preference learned about a stakeholder is shared across the department. It is memory-assisted improvement (chapter 9) lifted from the individual to the organisation.

At production scale

Org learning is how a team of agents compounds experience rather than each agent relearning in isolation. It carries the same risks as individual memory amplified — a bad lesson now affects a whole department — so the corroboration bar and the scope boundaries matter more, not less. The digital twin is the interesting frontier: simulating org decisions before making them.

Failure modes

  • Premature promotion. One agent's mistake becoming department doctrine.
  • Cross-department leakage. Finance lessons surfacing in engineering.

Choose it when

  • A team of agents shares a domain and should compound experience.

Agent societies and civilization

The experimental frontier: long-lived agent societies with membership, reputation, a shared constitution, and a blackboard — agents that persist and interact over time rather than assembling per goal.

Mechanism

The civilization layer models a persistent society: agents are members with reputation earned through outcomes; a constitution encodes inviolable principles; a governor enforces them; a blackboard and a bus enable communication; goals route by reputation and capability. It is a research-grade exploration of what happens when agents are durable citizens rather than ephemeral workers — closer to a multi-agent simulation than to the production loop.

At production scale

Rated low on both axes deliberately: the civilization layer is a genuine research direction, not a production workhorse. Its ideas — reputation-based routing, a machine-enforced constitution, persistent societies — are where multi-agent systems may go, but for production work the bounded team and department model above is what earns its keep. It is included because it is real code and a real part of the platform, and because it names the frontier honestly.

Failure modes

  • Emergent misbehaviour. Persistent agents developing unintended dynamics.
  • Reputation gaming. Agents optimising reputation over outcomes.
  • Unbounded society growth. Cost with no clear owner.

Choose it when

  • Research into persistent multi-agent systems.
  • Not for production workloads — use bounded teams.

What is missing here

  • Team eval and certification. Individual agents are evaluated; a formed team's effectiveness as a unit is harder to measure and not systematically gated.
  • Cross-department budgets. Budgets are per-goal and per-tenant; a department-level budget with its own accounting is thin.
  • Reputation grounded in evals. The civilization reputation signal is not yet tied to the eval scores that would make it trustworthy.
  • Org-structure change management. Reshaping departments is possible but there is no migration flow for the memory and access that move with them.
