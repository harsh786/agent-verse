Orientation

In a chatbot, hallucination means a wrong sentence. In an agent, it means a wrong action — and the four kinds are structurally different problems with different defences:

KindLooks likeDefence that actually works
Fabricated factA number or clause that is in no documentGrounding check per claim
Fabricated citationA plausible reference to a chunk that does not existOnly emit pipeline-assigned ids; verify
Fabricated toolA call to a tool or argument that does not existRegistry validation before dispatch
Fabricated success"Done" when nothing happenedVerify against tool results, not narration

The fourth is the dangerous one and the one prompt engineering cannot touch. An agent that reports success it did not achieve corrupts downstream state, satisfies its own verifier if the verifier reads its narration, and is only caught by checking evidence.

The defences

Retrieval grounding and the confidence floor

The cheapest anti-hallucination control is refusing to answer from context that scored below a calibrated threshold.

Mechanism

Before generation, check the retrieval result: is the top score above the floor, is there a meaningful gap between the best and the fifth candidate, did the query's key terms appear at all? Below threshold, the honest paths are to widen the search, try another source, or say the corpus does not contain the answer — not to hand the model three weak chunks and an instruction to be helpful.

Then instruct explicitly: answer only from the provided evidence; if it is insufficient, say so. That instruction only works if the pipeline actually gives the model the option to fail.

Worked example

query "what is the APAC revenue recognition policy?"
retrieval  top score 0.28 · floor 0.50 · gap 0.03 (flat distribution)
without a floor
  three weak chunks about global policy → confident answer about APAC
  that is a synthesis of documents which never mention APAC
with a floor
  "No APAC-specific policy is indexed. The global policy POL-204 may
   apply. Searched: policies, finance, legal."

At production scale

Most measured hallucinations in retrieval-augmented systems trace back to weak retrieval that nobody gated. Fixing the floor typically removes more hallucination than any change to the generation step, and it costs nothing at inference time.

Failure modes

  • No floor. The system always answers.
  • Uncalibrated floor. Either refuses too much or admits junk.
  • No "not found" affordance. The instruction says one thing, the pipeline forces another.

Choose it when

  • Always, and first.

Deterministic grounding

Extract the concrete things — numbers, ids, dates, quoted strings, URLs — and check each against the evidence by substring. Free, fast, and catches the majority of fabrications.

Mechanism

A regex pass pulls entity-shaped tokens out of the output, then each is checked for presence in the tool outputs and retrieved chunks. An invoice number, a total, an account id or a quoted clause either appears in the evidence or it was invented — no model judgement required. Only the claims that survive this pass (paraphrases and inferences) need an entailment model.

Two-pass ordering is the whole design: the cheap pass resolves most claims, so the expensive pass runs on a fraction of them.

Worked example

output  "Invoice INV-2024-8891 for ₹41,200 was settled on 12 March by
         Razorpay under contract C-2291."

extracted   INV-2024-8891 · 41200 · 2026-03-12 · Razorpay · C-2291
checked     INV-2024-8891  ✓ tool output step 2
            41200          ✓ tool output step 2
            2026-03-12     ✓ tool output step 2
            Razorpay       ✓ chunk [3]
            C-2291         ✗ NOT PRESENT anywhere    ← fabricated
action  annotate as ungrounded; step fails verification; replan or escalate

At production scale

Run this on every step and every final answer — it is a regex pass over a few kilobytes and it is the highest-yield check in the system. It is also the ideal online metric: cheap enough for 100% coverage, which makes it the fastest detector of a corpus or retrieval regression (chapter 10).

Escalate to the model-based check only for high-risk steps and unresolved claims. Checking everything with a model costs more than the generation did.

Failure modes

  • Formatting mismatch. "₹41,200" versus "41200.00" — normalise numbers and dates before comparing.
  • Paraphrase blindness. It cannot judge "roughly a third" against "31%"; that is what entailment is for.
  • Checking against the wrong evidence. Compare only against evidence actually in the prompt.

Choose it when

  • Always. This is the best value in the chapter.

Claim decomposition

Split the answer into atomic, independently checkable claims — because "the answer is 83% grounded" is not actionable and "claim 7 of 9 is unsupported" is.

Mechanism

Decompose into single-predicate statements, each carrying its span in the original text so a failure can be localised and, if necessary, the span removed or flagged. Claims are then classified: factual (must be grounded), inferential (must be marked as inference), and rhetorical (no grounding required). Only the first class gates the answer.

Worked example

"Refunds are accepted within 30 days, though damaged goods are exempt,
 so you should be fine claiming next week."

c1 factual     refunds accepted within 30 days        → [1] ✓
c2 factual     damaged goods exempt from the window   → [2] ✓
c3 inferential the user's claim next week qualifies   → depends on c1 and

action  c3 is an inference presented as advice — flagged, rewritten as
        "if your delivery was within the last 30 days, this would qualify"

At production scale

The claim class that causes real trouble is the inferential one delivered as fact — "so you should be fine". Distinguishing it and forcing it to be marked as conditional is a small change with a disproportionate effect on how much a user can rely on the output.

Failure modes

  • Over-decomposition. Twenty trivial claims per paragraph; check cost explodes.
  • Lost spans. A failed claim you cannot locate in the text.
  • Mis-classification. Advice treated as rhetoric and left unchecked.

Choose it when

  • Answers are multi-sentence and consequential.

Entailment (NLI) checking

For claims that survive the deterministic pass, ask a model a narrow question: does this evidence entail this claim, contradict it, or neither?

Mechanism

Pair each unresolved claim with its candidate evidence and classify the relation as entailment, contradiction, or neutral. The narrowness is what makes it reliable — it is a three-way classification on a short pair, not an open-ended judgement, so a small model does it well and cheaply. Contradiction is a stronger signal than neutral and should be treated more severely: neutral means unsupported, contradiction means the evidence says otherwise.

In AgentVerse

  • app/intelligence/nli_checker.pyNLIChecker
  • app/evals/attribution_verifier.pyAttributionVerifier — citation support
  • app/agent/grounding.pycheck_async() — the escalation path

Worked example

claim     "most disputes are resolved within a week"
evidence  "of 412 disputes, ۲۸۹ closed within 5 business days"
           (289/412 = 70%)
verdict   ENTAILED (0.86) — "most" is supported

claim     "disputes are usually resolved same-day"
evidence  same
verdict   CONTRADICTED (0.91) — treated as a hard failure, not merely
           unsupported

At production scale

Use a small dedicated model, not the generation model. This is a classification task where a compact model matches a large one and costs a fraction, and using the generator introduces self-preference. Batch the pairs — a dozen claim/evidence pairs in one call is normal.

Failure modes

  • Wrong evidence paired. A claim checked against an irrelevant chunk reads as neutral.
  • Neutral treated as fine. Unsupported is not supported.
  • Cost creep. Checking every claim rather than the unresolved ones.

Choose it when

  • Answers contain paraphrase and inference — which is most prose answers.

Citation verification

Check two things about every citation: that the id exists, and that the cited evidence supports the claim attached to it. Both fail in practice.

Mechanism

Existence is a lookup against the citation map the pipeline built — an id the model invented is rejected immediately. Support is the entailment check applied per citation. A citation that exists but does not support its claim is the subtler failure: it looks rigorous and is wrong, and it is common when a model attaches the nearest available id to a sentence it generated independently.

At production scale

Track citation precision — the share of citations that both exist and support their claim — as a product metric. It is the number that determines whether users learn to trust the citations or learn to ignore them, and once they ignore them the whole grounding apparatus stops delivering value.

Failure modes

  • Existence-only checking. Misses the attached-not-derived case.
  • Shipping unverified citations. Manufactured trust.
  • Stripping silently. The user sees a claim with no source and no warning.

Choose it when

  • Any output that cites.

Tool name and argument validation

The structural backstop: a call to a tool that is not in this tenant's registry never dispatches, and arguments are schema-validated before anything executes.

Mechanism

Four steps between the model's output and a real side effect. Extract the intended call from possibly-malformed output. Repair obvious argument problems — type coercion, defaults, dropping hallucinated fields. Validate the name against the live registry and the arguments against the tool's schema, including enums and formats. Classify risk and route accordingly.

This is the layer that makes prompt injection survivable: a model persuaded to call admin.delete_tenant still has to get past a registry that has no such tool for this tenant.

               "args":{"repo":"acme/api","confirm":"yes","force":true}}

            github.create_pr, github.merge_pr, deploy.trigger

At production scale

Never resolve a tool name from text the model read — only from the registry. That single rule closes the path from "a document told the agent to call X" to an actual call. And keep the offered tool list exactly equal to the permitted list: a tool visible in the prompt will eventually be called.

Track hallucinated-tool rate per model. It is a useful model-quality signal and it rises sharply when a prompt change makes the tool list confusing.

Failure modes

  • Dispatch before validation. The whole point.
  • Accepting unknown arguments. A pass-through that reaches the API.
  • Offering unauthorised tools. A prompt-level permission bypass.
  • Repair that changes intent. Coercion is fine; guessing a missing required argument is not.

Choose it when

  • Always. This is not optional in any tool-calling system.

Verifier checks against evidence

The only defence against fabricated success: judge the step by the tool results, never by the agent's account of what it did.

Mechanism

The verifier receives the success criterion and the raw evidence, and deliberately not the executor's reasoning. It answers a specific question — does this evidence satisfy this criterion — and returns a verdict with a diagnosis. Where the criterion is checkable deterministically (a record exists, a total matches), that check runs first and the model is only needed for the judgement calls.

Worked example

executor narration  "I have filed the amendment successfully."
tool result         {"error":"period locked","ack_no":null}

verifier shown narration only   → PASS   ← fabricated success accepted
verifier shown evidence only    → FAIL   "no acknowledgement number;
                                          error indicates the period is
                                          locked"  → replan or escalate

At production scale

This single design choice — evidence, not narration — prevents the most damaging agent failure there is, because fabricated success propagates: downstream steps assume the filing happened, the goal completes, and a human discovers it weeks later.

Calibrate the verifier. A verifier that passes almost everything has stopped being a control, and the only way to know is to measure it against labelled outcomes.

Failure modes

  • Narration in the verifier prompt. The verifier agrees with the actor.
  • No explicit criterion. Nothing to verify against.
  • Uncalibrated leniency. Verification theatre.

Choose it when

  • Always, and never let the actor's reasoning in.

Confidence estimation

A useful confidence number comes from measurable signals — agreement, grounding, retrieval strength — not from asking the model how sure it is.

Mechanism

Self-reported confidence is poorly calibrated and cheaply gamed. Better signals, combined: retrieval strength (top score and gap), grounding rate (share of claims supported), agreement across samples or verifiers, and historical success for this goal class and tool set. Combine into a score, then calibrate it against outcomes so that "0.8" actually means roughly 80% correct.

The output is only useful if something acts on it: below a threshold, escalate to consensus, to a stronger model, or to a human.

Worked example

signal                       value  weight
retrieval top score           0.88    0.3
retrieval gap (1st vs 5th)    0.31    0.1
grounding rate (claims)       1.00    0.4
self-consistency agreement    0.80    0.1

combined                      0.91  → proceed

same goal, weak variant: grounding 0.67, agreement 0.60 → 0.68
→ below the 0.75 escalation threshold → consensus verification

At production scale

Calibration is what makes this worth building: an uncalibrated confidence score encourages exactly the wrong behaviour, because people trust the high numbers. Measure predicted confidence against observed correctness on a sample, and publish the curve rather than the number.

Failure modes

  • Model self-report. Uniformly high and uninformative.
  • Uncalibrated combination. Arbitrary weights producing arbitrary confidence.
  • Confidence with no consequence. A number nobody routes on.

Choose it when

  • You need selective escalation rather than gating everything.

Output anomaly detection

Catch outputs that are structurally unlike what this agent normally produces — sudden length changes, unexpected languages, secret-shaped strings, refusal spikes.

Mechanism

Statistical rather than semantic: track distributions of output length, language, format compliance, refusal rate, and the presence of credential-shaped or PII-shaped strings. Flag deviations. It catches what claim-checking misses — a compromised prompt, a model change, a template that started leaking internal content — because those show up as shape changes before they show up as wrong facts.

At production scale

Anomaly detection is the layer that catches problems you did not anticipate, which is why it belongs alongside the specific checks rather than instead of them. The refusal-rate signal in particular is a fast detector of prompt regressions that no eval covered.

Secret-shaped strings in output should be a hard block, not an alert. There is no benign reason for a credential pattern in an agent's answer.

Failure modes

  • Baselines too tight. Alert fatigue, then ignored alerts.
  • Global baselines. Per-agent distributions differ enormously.
  • Alerting instead of blocking. For credentials and PII, block.

Choose it when

  • Any production agent with external output.

Escalation under uncertainty

When the checks disagree or confidence is low, the answer is not "try harder" — it is consensus, then a human, and never silent completion.

Mechanism

An ordered ladder with a fail-closed end. Low confidence on a low-risk goal: retry with corrective retrieval. Low confidence on a high-risk goal: consensus verification across independent models. Disagreement in consensus: human approval. Human unavailable within the deadline: the goal ends as requiring attention, not as complete.

The last step is the one that matters. Any ladder whose final rung is "proceed anyway" is not a control.

Worked example

goal  "file the amendment"  risk=critical  confidence 0.68

1 corrective retrieval → confidence 0.74, still below 0.85 for critical
2 consensus            → primary PASS · cross-model FAIL · judge FAIL
3 majority FAIL        → waiting_human with all three rationales
4 no approval in 24h   → goal ends as needs_attention, not complete
                          and definitely not filed

At production scale

Escalation rate is a tuning signal, not a failure: too low and you are letting uncertain actions through; too high and the approval queue becomes a backlog nobody services. Watch both the rate and the queue depth, and remember that an unserviced approval queue is an outage that pages nobody.

Failure modes

  • Escalation to nowhere. A queue with no owner.
  • Proceed-on-timeout. Fail closed instead.
  • Escalating everything. Approvers stop reading.
  • Losing the rationales. The human needs the disagreement, not just the verdict.

Choose it when

  • Any irreversible or regulated action.

Observability for hallucination debugging

To debug a hallucination you need four things: what was retrieved, what was in the prompt, what the model returned, and which check fired. Anything less is speculation.

Mechanism

Correlate by goal and step: the retrieval trace (strategy, filters, candidates, scores), the prompt recipe (variant, context item ids, budget decisions), the model trace (model, parameters, tokens), and the check results (grounding per claim, citation verification, validation failures). One correlation id ties them together so a single query answers "why did it say that".

            grounding flag that did not gate. Two fixes, both specific.

At production scale

The value of this is measured in minutes-to-root-cause. With traces, the above takes one query and produces two concrete fixes. Without them, it becomes a debate about model quality that ends in "let's use a bigger model", which costs more and fixes nothing.

Sample full prompts and retain them only for failures; keep recipes for everything. That keeps the storage and privacy cost proportionate while preserving the debugging path.

Failure modes

  • No correlation id. Four separate logs that cannot be joined.
  • Warnings that do not gate. The check fired and nothing happened — exactly the case above.
  • Traces without scores. You see what was retrieved, not why.

Choose it when

  • Before your first hallucination complaint.

What is missing here

  • A single calibrated confidence score. The signals exist separately; nothing combines and calibrates them into one number that drives escalation platform-wide.
  • Contradiction detection across sources. Nothing flags that two retrieved documents disagree, which is a common cause of confidently wrong synthesis.
  • Grounding for non-text output. Claims derived from a chart or a screenshot have no equivalent of the deterministic check.
  • User-facing uncertainty. Confidence is recorded internally but rarely surfaced in the answer, which is where it would do the most good.
  • Hallucination rate as a published SLO. Measured internally, not offered as a tenant-visible service level.
